Supplier Cost-Benefit Analysis & Validation

Materials sourcing for 3D-printed dental prosthetics

Role
Engineering Research & Development Intern
Organization
Spectrum Killian Dental Lab Alliance (SKDLA)
Period
Jul 2025 - Oct 2025
Tag
RD-01

The problem

The lab bought a consumable staining material for its 3D-printed dental prosthetics from an established supplier, at a price that had never been challenged. An alternative supplier offered a comparable material for less. But a lower sticker price is not a decision: switching a production material puts product quality at risk, and for a patient-facing medical product quality cannot be allowed to regress. My task was to determine, with evidence, whether the alternative was genuinely equivalent and genuinely worth adopting.

Constraints

  • Quality could not regress. The end product is worn by patients. A cost saving that compromised quality was not a saving.
  • A live production environment. Any test had to run without disrupting the technicians' ongoing work.
  • A decision that had to survive scrutiny. The recommendation would drive real spend, so it had to be defensible to the technicians who use the material and the management who pay for it.
  • Proprietary boundaries. Supplier terms and internal costs were confidential, so the work had to be documented in a way that captured the method without exposing specifics.

Approach

I treated it as a trade study, not a price comparison. I built the cost side from total cost of use rather than unit price alone, so that anything the cheaper material might cost elsewhere, in waste, rework, or slower processing, would show up rather than hide. On the performance side, I defined what "equivalent quality" had to mean in measurable terms before running anything, so the result could not be argued with after the fact.

What I delivered

  • A side-by-side validation test that I organized and led, putting the current material and the alternative in front of both the technicians who apply it and the management who judge the result, under the same conditions. Structuring it as a direct comparison made the outcome evidence rather than opinion.
  • A comparative report that laid cost and quality out together and made a clear recommendation. This is what the sourcing decision was actually made on.
  • Standard operating procedures and test protocols, so the change could be adopted consistently across multiple technicians instead of living in one person's head.

Result

The alternative met the quality bar in the side-by-side, and the analysis put a number on the opportunity: more than a 30% material cost reduction, into six figures of projected annual savings. The lab ultimately chose not to switch suppliers, but that call was made on evidence rather than assumption, which was the point of the work. The standard operating procedures I wrote outlasted the question, giving the team a repeatable way to evaluate a material change across technicians.

What I took from it

The habit I built here is the one I now use on propulsion hardware: decide what "good enough" has to mean, in measurable terms, before you run the test, and build the cost case from total cost of use rather than the number on the invoice. The cheapest or highest-performing option on paper is frequently not the right selection once the full picture is in. It is the same discipline behind how I approach component selection on a fluid system.
